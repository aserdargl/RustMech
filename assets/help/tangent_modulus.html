Estimate tangent modulus from a datasheet

E, yield strength, tensile strength and fracture elongation do not uniquely determine hardening. The calculator provides a rough starting value for sensitivity studies, not a measured material property.

Enter all stresses in the displayed unit. Enter elongation as a percentage: 20 means 20%, not 0.20. Inputs should describe the solid base material, with matching temperature, build direction and processing condition; do not use the apparent stress–strain response of a porous lattice as the base material.

Approximation used

ef = fracture elongation / 100
ey ≈ yield strength / E
Et, estimate = (tensile strength − yield strength) / (ef − ey)

This constructs an artificial straight line from yield to tensile strength at fracture elongation. These last two values generally belong to different points of the test. It is an engineering secant proxy, adopted as an approximation to the application's true-stress/total-logarithmic-strain slope.

Fracture elongation usually exceeds uniform elongation at maximum load, so substituting it tends to reduce the engineering hardening slope. This is not a guaranteed lower bound for the actual true-strain modulus. A 0.2% proof strength is also only an approximation to the model's initial yield.

Example

E = 200000 MPa, yield = 250 MPa, tensile strength = 400 MPa, fracture elongation = 20%:
Et, estimate = 150 / (0.20 − 0.00125) ≈ 754.717 MPa.

What the solver needs

RustMech's Et is the post-yield slope of true stress versus total logarithmic strain. It must satisfy 0 ≤ Et < E. The solver adapter converts it to the plastic hardening modulus H = E Et / (E − Et); do not enter H in the Et field.

Do not use fracture elongation to convert UTS to true stress. The usual engineering-to-true conversion applies only before necking. With measured uniform elongation or a full tensile curve, fit the relevant pre-necking true stress–strain range or use the True stress–strain (MISO) model instead.

Bilinear isotropic hardening does not predict fracture or cap stress at UTS. For compression, validate the tensile-based estimate against compression data. Compare several plausible Et values to see whether the result depends strongly on this uncertain input.
